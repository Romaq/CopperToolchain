## 🤖 CLAUDE · Turn 32 · 2026-11-03

I'm not a lawyer, so this is information to help you decide, not legal advice. Several licenses fit what you asked for, and the choice turns on one question, below.

### Your two criteria

I read "does not grant me liability" as "the license doesn't make me liable": it should say the work comes as-is, with no warranty and no liability for you. I read "freedom to use and distribute without restriction" as no conditions on users, not even a requirement to keep your name attached.

### Licenses that meet both

| License | Conditions on users | Disclaimer of warranty and liability | Form | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Unlicense** | None | Yes | Public-domain dedication, with a fallback license | OSI-approved |
| **CC0** | None | Yes | Public-domain dedication, with a fallback license | Not OSI-approved; expressly grants no patent rights |
| **0BSD** | None | Yes | A plain license grant, not a dedication | OSI-approved |

Close misses:

- **MIT** is the most familiar permissive license, but users must keep your copyright notice and the license text, which is a small restriction.
- **MIT No Attribution (MIT-0)** removes that condition and also exists.
- **WTFPL** is informal and lacks a warranty disclaimer, so it fails your second criterion.

### The question that decides it

Do you want to **declare the work public domain**, or simply **grant everyone every right**?

- **Public-domain dedications** (Unlicense, CC0) state the intent to relinquish the work. Some jurisdictions don't let authors do that. CC0 was created for such places, such as continental Europe. The Unlicense describes itself as having a fallback license for when the waiver doesn't apply, but critics think it is poorly drafted, and the OSI approved it anyway in 2020. That is a contested point, so treat it as a difference of expert opinion.
- **A license grant** (0BSD) gives permission everywhere without any public-domain claim. It is a grant with no strings attached plus a warranty disclaimer, and it is OSI-approved. One commentator therefore prefers it for software over the Unlicense. The Unlicense's own approval and FSF endorsement are the counterweight, so people disagree.

### Other things to weigh

1. **Patents:** CC0 explicitly says it grants no patent licenses, which led Fedora to stop allowing it for software code (it still allows non-code). The Unlicense and 0BSD say nothing on patents. This only matters if you ever hold patents.
2. **Institutional acceptance:** according to Wikipedia, Google does not accept contributions to projects under public-domain-style licenses like the Unlicense and CC0, but does accept them for 0BSD. If professional contributors might touch your code, recognition matters.
3. **Code versus documents:** this repo holds scripts, but also documents and prompts. You can use one license for everything, which fits your "keep it simple" rule, or one for code and CC0 for documents. I'd keep it to one unless a reason appears.
4. **Timing:** you can add or change a license at any time. As I understand it, copies someone already received keep the terms they got, while your later versions can use new terms.

### Putting it in place

On github.com: Add file → Create new file → name it `LICENSE`. A "Choose a license template" button appears. I haven't checked which of these GitHub's picker lists. If 0BSD isn't there, copy its text from the SPDX license list or opensource.org, so it matches the standard wording exactly. Your `sync.sh` already includes `LICENSE`, and the privacy check isn't affected.

Whichever you choose, record the choice and your reason in `docs/decisions/`, so DJV22 can see the thinking and not just the file.
